A letter from Quadia

Before you give one more year to being the strong one.

Three minutes to read. Written for the woman everyone leans on, by a woman who used to be her.

✦

Sis,

I know what your mornings look like, because they were mine. Awake before everyone, already listing. The lunches, the deadlines, the mother who needs calling, the friend who needs holding, the body that gets whatever minutes are left, which is to say, none. You do it beautifully. Everyone says so. Dependable. Unbreakable. The rock.

Nobody asks the rock how she is doing. And somewhere along the way you stopped asking yourself.

I spent years wearing exhaustion like a wedding ring, proof of how much I loved my family, my work, my people. I slept and woke up tired anyway. I sat down and my mind kept running. I told myself I would rest when things calmed down, and things never once calmed down on their own.

Then I did the thing I was raised to believe was selfish. I rested first. And nothing fell apart, except the lie.

I did not fall behind. I became sharper, softer, more successful, more present, more myself. Depletion had been quietly taxing everything I built, and I had been calling the tax virtue. Unlearning that took me years. I wrote The EXHALE so it would not take you as long.

It is seven short reflections, one each morning for a week, about ten minutes with your coffee. Each day ends with a few reflection questions and a private place to write what they stir, kept on your device and nowhere else. It is free, it is private, and it does not add a single task to your list. It only asks one thing of you: to stop treating your tiredness as a personal failure, and to start treating it as information.

You have been strong for everyone. This is for the woman underneath.

Quadia
Founder of BLOOM · Reinvention coach for women 40+

My story

Before BLOOM, there was the strong one

If you have ever been her, this will feel familiar.

The beginning · eldest of three

The girl who learned to be strong

Strength wasn’t something I chose. It chose me. And then it simply became my identity.

I was born the eldest of three children, and although I didn’t know it then, from an early age I began to learn the role that would shape much of my life. Responsibility arrived early. Taking care of my brother while my mom worked at night. Chaperoning my sister’s field trips. I became the one who noticed what needed to be done, who stepped in before someone had to ask, and who quietly learned that being dependable was something to be proud of.

Over time, I stopped distinguishing between who I was and who I needed to be. The strong one. The one who could handle it. The one people called on. That lesson followed me into adulthood.

My twenties · proving I was worthy

The start of erosion

The sacrifices were small at first. That’s how you end up on your own back burner.

Like many Black women, I was determined to break generational patterns, to prove I was worthy. I worked. Then I worked through school. I joined Public Allies, led programs that helped women, and put in 80-hour weeks because I thought that’s what a strong woman does. I was making more money than older family members, so when they asked, I gave. Sacrifice yourself for the sake of others — that’s what strong women do.

From the outside, my life looked successful, and in many ways it was. I travelled. I dressed up. I was doing me. But somewhere along the way, my own needs started quietly slipping to the bottom of the list. Not getting dressed for holidays because I was “cooking all day.” Staying later than my co-workers. Letting others convince me otherwise when I said no. A skipped meal. Working through exhaustion. Believing rest could wait until everything else was finished.

The problem is that everything is never finished.

The building years · wife, mother, leader

Living without room

“What do I need?” and “What still needs to be done?” are two very different questions.

Responsibilities multiplied, and so did my capacity to meet them. What didn’t grow was the space I had left for myself. I married. I became a mother. I pursued higher education — as a Black woman, you gotta get that degree. I built a meaningful career and became exceptionally skilled at caring for everyone and everything entrusted to me. Family. Work. Leadership. Community.

I stopped asking “What do I need?” and started asking “What still needs to be done?” Eventually I looked around at the life I had built and admitted something difficult: I was tired. I was dependable, I could be counted on and called on, and I had created a life that looked good — but I was tired. Doomscrolling became my getaway, especially at night. Rest was needed, but not welcomed.

At forty-six

The year everything changed

Rest is not the reward you receive after doing enough. It is one of the conditions that allows you to live well in the first place.

At forty-six, I made a decision that would eventually become the foundation of BLOOM. I gave myself permission to stop operating on autopilot. Not because I wanted less from life. Because I wanted more from it.

I spent two years getting back to myself. I became curious about why I felt chronically depleted despite doing everything a successful woman is supposed to do. I studied the nervous system, behavior, rest, stress, attention, identity, and the ways Black women are taught to be “the strong one.” I experimented with new rhythms: quieter mornings, healthier food, movement, hydration, prayer, reflection, and breath practices. Little by little, I discovered something I had never been taught.

2025 · now

Why BLOOM exists

BLOOM is my answer to that question.

In 2025, I returned from a two-week vacation and asked myself: how can vacation Quadia become everyday Quadia? Vacation felt like an escape, and that day I decided I wanted a life I didn’t need to escape from. BLOOM was created to help women build lives they no longer need to escape from.

The practices inside BLOOM are simple, but they are not small. Every exhale creates a little more room. Every moment of rest gives you space. Every act of trust loosens the grip of survival and makes space for choice. I don’t believe women need fixing. I believe many of us have spent so many years carrying what life required that we have forgotten to ask whether the life we’re carrying still has room for us.
